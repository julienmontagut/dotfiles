# Bet — step 2 of 10

## Purpose

Make the decision to continue or stop mechanical rather than emotional. A bet without a kill criterion becomes retroactive justification.

## The three numbers

**Appetite** — how much time this is worth. A judgement about budget, not about the work. Fixed. Scope bends to fit the appetite; the appetite never bends to fit the scope. This replaces estimation entirely.

**Signal** — the smallest observable behaviour that would justify continuing. Behaviour must cost the observed person something: their data, their time, their money, their reputation. Opinions, stated intent, and enthusiasm do not count.

**Deadline** — the date the keep/kill decision gets made. Not the date the work finishes.

## Procedure

1. State the appetite first, before any scoping. "This is worth three weeks."
2. Write the signal as an absolute, observable count when the population is small: "5 of the 15 people I show it to load their own real data within two weeks." Small numbers are fine — you are looking for strong signal, not statistical significance.
3. Set the deadline.
4. Write all three down before any work starts. A bet written afterwards is a rationalisation.

## Done when

Appetite, signal and deadline exist in writing and a stranger could apply them without asking you.

## Anti-patterns

- Estimating instead of setting appetite.
- A signal made of opinions ("they said they loved it").
- Extending the deadline. At the deadline you ship or kill — never extend.
- No customers yet, so skipping the bet. At zero customers everything is a bet; the risk is at its maximum, not its minimum.

## Demand-side bets

With no customers, the riskiest assumption is not "can I build it" but "will anyone relevant see it and care". Order the first bets accordingly, none of which need a product:

- Reachability: can I get 20 people in one narrow segment into a conversation?
- Problem: do 5 of them describe the problem unprompted, before I pitch?
- Urgency: will 2 of them give me access to their real workflow or data?
